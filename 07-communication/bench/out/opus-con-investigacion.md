The "split-attention effect" is the drop in learning that appears when someone has to join in their head two sources that only work together, for example a diagram here and its explanation there; for technical documentation it means putting the text inside the diagram or the code, not next to it or on another page.

- The cost isn't in the content, it's in the back-and-forth. The source puts it this way: "Learners must mentally integrate two sources of information in order to understand the solution, a process that yields a high cognitive load and hampers learning." That mental integration consumes attention that's no longer left for understanding.
- Analogy: assembling furniture with the steps on one sheet and the parts diagram on another. The steps sheet is your text; the diagram, your figure; your head is what goes back and forth. Where it breaks: both furniture sheets fit open on the table at once, while on a screen the reader scrolls and always loses sight of one of the two.
- Duplicating isn't the answer either. If the diagram already makes sense on its own, adding the full text next to it makes the result worse: "only presenting the diagram was superior to presenting both sources of information together." First check whether one of the two sources is redundant; only if neither is, merge them into one.
- The part you control is the presentation, not the topic. Extraneous load (the effort added by the form of presentation, not the real difficulty of the subject) is determined by "how the information is presented and what the learner is required to do by the instructional procedure." A hard topic stays hard; the back-and-forth between sources is on you.
- Contrast: all three quotes come from the same article, so they agree by construction, not by agreement between independent authors. None of the three talks about technical documentation.
