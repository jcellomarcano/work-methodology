The "split-attention effect" happens when a reader has to mentally integrate two sources of information to understand something; that integration consumes so much cognitive capacity that it hampers learning.

Your mind processes information within limits. When a diagram requires you to read text at the same time, the integration consumes almost all that capacity, leaving little for comprehension. A diagram without text is more effective than a diagram with an explanation next to it.

**Analogy.** It's like following a road map while someone gives you directions out loud. If they talk while you read the map, you understand less of both. If they wait until you finish reading a section, you understand better. Here, divided attention (reading + listening) is the symptom; your limited cognitive capacity is the cause.

**Implication for technical documentation.** Your presentation determines the load. Being correct isn't enough; you must avoid dividing the reader's attention. A short label on the diagram beats a paragraph of explanation next to it. Even extraneous load, how you present and what you demand, matters as much as the content.

The three sources agree: integration consumes capacity, the diagram alone is better, presentation determines load. No contradiction between them.

**Next step:** audit your documentation: in which sections do you ask readers to integrate two sources? Can they be presented one after the other instead?

**Not tested:** whether this pattern holds the same for technical readers versus those without technical experience, or whether there's a complexity threshold where grouping the information helps.
