The "split-attention effect" is the cost of having to join in your head two sources that only work together: a diagram that isn't explained and a text you can't see.

- Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas (2019) describe it this way: "Learners must mentally integrate two sources of information in order to understand the solution, a process that yields a high cognitive load and hampers learning."
- Adding material doesn't always help. The same source, on the redundancy effect: "only presenting the diagram was superior to presenting both sources of information together."
- The problem isn't in the topic, it's in the format. The same authors attribute extraneous load to "how the information is presented and what the learner is required to do by the instructional procedure."
- Think of furniture you have to assemble. The parts sheet is the diagram; the steps sheet, on another table, is the text. Every trip between the two tables costs memory. Unlike the furniture, your reader can't push the tables together: you fix that separation when you lay out the page.
- In technical documentation that's three concrete changes: put the label inside the diagram, not in a separate caption; keep the command and its output side by side, in the same visible block; and if the diagram already makes sense on its own, delete the prose that repeats it instead of adding it.
