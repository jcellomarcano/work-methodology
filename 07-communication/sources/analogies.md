## 2. What works

- Analogies transfer relational structure, not surface traits: N2, N3, N4.
- They measurably raise problem-solving and scores: 10% without an analogy, 30% with a story, 75-80% with a hint (N5, N7); +2.16 points, "10.8% improvement" in a computer science class (N11); d = 0.50 across 57 case-comparison experiments (N8).
- Explicit mapping is what unlocks the gain: spontaneous transfer is ~30%; the explicit hint adds ~50 points (N5, N7); "Map similarities" is a required step in TWA (N10, N11).
- Familiar base domain: "a concept well understood by the learner" (N11); "Cue retrieval of analog concept" (N10); N34.
- Several analogies or a comparison beat a single one: 52% versus 30% with two stories (N7); N6; N13; N12.
- The concrete is remembered better than the abstract: N24, N25, N30.
- Personal relevance helps engagement and recall (moderate evidence): N31, N32, N33.

## 3. What breaks

- Overextension and getting locked into a single analogy: N12 (title: "analogy-induced misconception"), N13 ("double-edged swords"), N14.
- Poorly designed analogies confuse: N11.
- Surface resemblances disguised as analogy: N4.
- The listener does not retrieve the analogy on their own: ~30% spontaneous transfer (N5, N7).
- The expert's blind spot (curse of knowledge): N26, N28.
- LLM-generated analogies can be wrong and memorable: N35, N32; readers misjudge their accuracy, a shorter analogy was perceived as less accurate "Despite no errors in either" (N34).
- Personalization mismatch: the benefit "may only be realized when students' degree of quantitative engagement with their out-of-school interests matches the depth at which the personalized problems are written" (N31).

## 4. Rules a writer can follow (derived from the citations)

1. Map the relation, not the appearance: choose a base whose relations (cause and effect, flow, constraint) mirror the target's (N2, N3, N4).
2. State the mapping explicitly ("X in the analogy is Y in the system"); without a hint, transfer is ~30% (N5, N7, N10).
3. Name where the analogy breaks down as a required step (N10, N11, N13).
4. Choose a base the reader already understands (N11, N34).
5. For complex concepts, use more than one analogy and invite comparison (N6, N7, N12, N13); the evidence does not support "one analogy per concept" as a general rule: it supports one explicit analogy at a time and several for complex material.
6. Prefer concrete, imaginable examples over abstract rephrasing (N24, N25, N30).
7. Assume you suffer from the curse of knowledge; test the explanation on a non-expert reader (N26, N28, N29).
8. Use "explain it to a novice" as a self-check, not as a Feynman quote: the only documented anecdote is Goodstein's (N20); the four-step "technique" is a 2011 Scott Young construction (N16-N19, N22).
9. When drawing on the reader's interests, match the depth to their actual engagement; a superficial veneer can fail (N31, N32).
10. Verify every LLM-generated analogy before publishing it; perceived accuracy is not a proxy (N32, N34, N35).
